Albert Einstein

We begin our semester with relativity. Therefore, to investigate more the non-objectivity in science, let's consider arguably the most famous physicist in history: Albert Einstein.

Albert Einstein revolutionized our understanding of the universe through his description of Special and General Relativity, which allowed the description of behaviors of objects at the smallest and largest scales of our universe.

Einstein was a Jewish man raised in Germany through the rise of the Nazi party. This background shaped his outlook on life, and his scientific contributions.

Einstein's Travel Journal

Before emigrating to the United States, Einstein had already achieved fame and had the opportunity to travel the world. Despite only spending a few days in China, his travel journal is full of racist themes and beliefs about the nature of all Chinese people. It is likely that Einstein harbored these negative beliefs througout his life despite future advocacy efforts.

Einstein's Political Impact

Albert Einstein, dismayed by the rise of anti-Semitic sentiments in Germany, emigrated to the United States in 1933, just as Adolf Hitler ascended to the chancellorship. His decision to leave Germany marked a significant turning point in his life and career, leading him to become a prominent figure in academia and public discourse in the United States.

When physicists got word of the discovery of nuclear fission in Germany, Einstein co-wrote a letter to the president of the United States outlining the potential severity of this discovery:

Einstein's letter to the United States President, F.D. Roosevelt, warning him of the possibility of a new, dangerous form of weapon based on the quantum propeties of nuceli, and the possibility that scientists in Nazi Germany could build such a weapon.

This letter led to the American development of the atomic bomb at Los Alomos National Laboratory in New Mexico. Once the United States government used this weapon of mass destruction, Einstein and other scientists, even those who had a hand in building the atomic bombs, became very vocal about nuclear disarmament.

In view of the fact that in any future world war nuclear weapons will certainly be employed, and that such weapons threaten the continued existence of mankind, we urge the governments of the world to realize, and to acknowledge publicly, that their purpose cannot be furthered by a world war, and we urge them, consequently, to find peaceful means for the settlement of all matters of dispute between them.

-Percy W. Bridgman, Albert Einstein, Leopold Infeld, Frederic Joliot-Curie, Herman J. Muller, Linus Pauling, Cecil F. Powell, Joseph Rotblat, Bertrand Russell, Hideki Yukawa

Einstein's "Biggest Blunder"

When Einstein was developing his theory of General Relativity, he realized that this mathematics indicated that the universe was expanding. Since that couldn't be true, he added a "cosmological constant" to his equations to remove this conclusion. This was later found to exactly encode the rate of expansion of the universe. Western ideology advanced from a "Earth-first" perspective, considering the earth a stationary obect at the center of a stationary universe.

In Einstein's later years, quantum mechanics was creating another foundational shift in our outlook of the workings of the universe. In a similar grasp onto his religious deterministic beliefs, Einstein was reluctant to accept the new ideas of quantum mechanics. He was famously quoted as saying "God doesn't play dice with the universe".

What if a Hindu born in India developed General Relativity? In many Eastern religions, an expanding universe has been a common outlook for thousands of years. This may have been a "biggest discovery" instead of a biggest blunder!

What if a Buddhist born in Sri Lanka was contemplating quantum mechanics? Buddhist philosophies such as emptiness and impermanence lend themselves well to the behaviors of quantum mechanics. In fact, nowadays, quantum phenomena are often referenced in Buddhist teachings and disussions about Buddhist philosophy.

Einstein and the Civil Rights Movement

Experiencing religious discrimination growing up in Germany during the rise of Nazi power, Einstein was not quiet about the racial discrimination he observed in the United States. He protested the injustice of the Scotsboro Boys trial. He used his platform to bring light to social issues, which angered some of his contemporary physicists such as Max Born.

There is separation of colored people from white people in the United States...That separation is not a disease of colored people. It is a disease of white people. I do not intend to be quiet about it.

-Albert Einstein, Lincoln University, 1946
