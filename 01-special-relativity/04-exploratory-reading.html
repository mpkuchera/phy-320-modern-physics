Albert Einstein

We begin our semester with relativity. Therefore, to investigate more the non-objectivity in science, let's consider arguably the most famous physicist in history: Albert Einstein.

Albert Einstein revolutionized our understanding of the universe through his description of Special and General Relativity, which allowed the description of behaviors of objects at the smallest and largest scales of our universe.

Einstein was a Jewish man raised in Germany through the rise of the Nazi party. This background shaped his outlook on life, and his scientific contributions.

Einstein's Travel Journal

Before emigrating to the United States, Einstein had already achieved fame and had the opportunity to travel the world. Despite only spending a few days in China, his travel journal includes racist themes and broad negative claims about Chinese people. The journals complicate the public image of Einstein as a humanitarian and remind us that scientific brilliance does not erase personal prejudice.

Einstein's Political Impact

Albert Einstein, dismayed by the rise of anti-Semitic sentiments in Germany, emigrated to the United States in 1933, just as Adolf Hitler ascended to the chancellorship. His decision to leave Germany marked a significant turning point in his life and career, leading him to become a prominent figure in academia and public discourse in the United States.

When physicists got word of the discovery of nuclear fission in Germany, Einstein signed a letter, drafted with input from Leo Szilard and others, to the president of the United States outlining the potential severity of this discovery:

Einstein's letter to the United States President, F.D. Roosevelt, warning him of the possibility of a new, dangerous form of weapon based on the quantum properties of nuclei, and the possibility that scientists in Nazi Germany could build such a weapon.

This letter helped spur United States government attention that contributed to the Manhattan Project and the development of the atomic bomb at Los Alamos National Laboratory in New Mexico. Once the United States government used this weapon of mass destruction, Einstein and other scientists, even those who had a hand in building the atomic bombs, became very vocal about nuclear disarmament.

In view of the fact that in any future world war nuclear weapons will certainly be employed, and that such weapons threaten the continued existence of mankind, we urge the governments of the world to realize, and to acknowledge publicly, that their purpose cannot be furthered by a world war, and we urge them, consequently, to find peaceful means for the settlement of all matters of dispute between them.

-Percy W. Bridgman, Albert Einstein, Leopold Infeld, Frederic Joliot-Curie, Herman J. Muller, Linus Pauling, Cecil F. Powell, Joseph Rotblat, Bertrand Russell, Hideki Yukawa

Einstein's "Biggest Blunder"

When Einstein was developing his theory of General Relativity, he realized that this mathematics indicated that the universe was not necessarily static. Since an evolving universe seemed impossible to many scientists at the time, he added a "cosmological constant" to his equations to allow a static universe. Later cosmology gave the cosmological constant new relevance in descriptions of dark energy and cosmic acceleration. Western scientific traditions had long been shaped by "Earth-first" and static-universe perspectives, even after astronomy displaced Earth from the center of the cosmos.

In Einstein's later years, quantum mechanics was creating another foundational shift in our outlook of the workings of the universe. Influenced by his belief that nature should be deterministic, Einstein was reluctant to accept some of the new ideas of quantum mechanics. He was famously quoted as saying "God doesn't play dice with the universe".

Relativity itself asks us to abandon the idea of a single privileged frame of reference in physics. This reading asks a parallel question about scientific culture: whose frames of reference have historically been treated as central?

What if a thinker raised in a philosophical tradition where cyclical, evolving, or expansive universes were familiar had developed General Relativity? Might the same mathematics have felt less like a blunder and more like a discovery?

What if a thinker shaped by Buddhist philosophical ideas such as emptiness and impermanence was contemplating quantum mechanics? These traditions do not automatically predict quantum physics, but they can offer different conceptual starting points for thinking about uncertainty, relationality, and change. In fact, nowadays, quantum phenomena are often referenced in Buddhist teachings and discussions about Buddhist philosophy.

Dr. Kuchera getting schooled in a traditional debate on scientific topics with Buddhist monks in India.

Einstein and the Civil Rights Movement

Experiencing religious discrimination growing up in Germany during the rise of Nazi power, Einstein was not quiet about the racial discrimination he observed in the United States. He protested the injustice of the Scottsboro Boys trial. He used his platform to bring light to social issues, which angered some of his contemporary physicists such as Max Born.

There is separation of colored people from white people in the United States...That separation is not a disease of colored people. It is a disease of white people. I do not intend to be quiet about it.

-Albert Einstein, Lincoln University, 1946

Einstein made these remarks during a visit to Lincoln University, a historically Black university in Pennsylvania. Read more about Einstein's civil rights advocacy.

Reflection

Your personal reflection is an important component of your personal trajectory as a scientist. You will be graded only on completion, but please take the time to sincerely reflect on the following questions. I will use the collective responses to help me design better materials in the future.

  1. As we go through this course, we will find that some of the traditionally celebrated figures in physics have taken actively racist actions. To what extent should a scientist's personal views, actions, and social context shape how we teach or evaluate their scientific work?
  2. How might your own experiences or viewpoints influence your approaches to studying or doing science?
  3. Relativity challenges the idea of a single privileged physical perspective. Are there useful parallels, or limits to the parallel, when thinking about perspective in science and society?
  4. How are ethics and science related? Are the paces at which they progress intertwined?
  5. Should there be history of physics/science courses incorporated into the required classes of the physics major at Davidson?
